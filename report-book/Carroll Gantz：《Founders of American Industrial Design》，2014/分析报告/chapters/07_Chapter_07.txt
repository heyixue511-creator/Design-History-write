## Labor Design and Design Organization With all the publicity and attention focused on the inevitable march of modern technology and design, as well as on the high unemployment levels, strikes and labor disputes of the time, it was perhaps inevitable that industrial design would become involved with government and the intensive labor activism of the Depression era. In April 1935, the Works Progress Administration (WPA) was created by an act of Congress upon the order of President Franklin Roosevelt as part of the Second New Deal. At the time, there were 20 million persons on relief. In order to employ the unemployed, the act provided \$4.9 billion in funds for the construction of roads, bridges, and local public buildings, but it also employed musicians, artists, actors and writers in public works. A number of artist/designers, including some mentioned in this book, took advantage of WPA grants to paint murals in post offices, universities, and other public buildings. In July, the Wagner National Labor Relations Act validated union authority. By August, the Social Security Act provided retirement benefits, the first federal assistance to the elderly. In September, the Hoover Dam (named after Herbert Hoover, president when it was begun in 1931) was dedicated, creating Lake Mead, the world's largest reservoir at the time, with President Roosevelt presiding. The Roosevelt administration quickly renamed it Boulder Dam, but the confusion between names continued. In 1947, the name would be changed back to Hoover Dam. On December 2, 1935, in New York, a frigid and blustery day, several hundred men and women lined up outside the 35th Street offices of the WPA for a chance to enroll in the Design Laboratory, a pioneering school sponsored by the Federal Art Project (FAP), one of the WPA's cultural projects. It was the first institution to offer free, comprehensive education in modernist design. Its brochure claimed it had been “created to supply a hitherto unfulfilled and pressing need in America" for people with skills to devise, package, and promote consumer products that were styled to sell, thereby stimulating economic recovery. Clearly, this noble purpose was inspired by the incredible success of industrial designers in doing precisely that over the previous eight years. By emphasizing “coordination in the study ofesthetics, industrial products, machine fabrication, and merchandizing," the school would "train designers, not specialized craftsmen, by correlating thorough instruction in the general principles of design and the fine arts with shop practice." Two hundred twenty people enrolled that day, more that the school's administration anticipated, and there were still hundreds more on a waiting list The school was the result of political action by the Depression-era avant-garde, the unprecedented public arts bureaucracy of the New Deal, the militant industrial unionism of the Congress of Industrial Organizations (CIO), which had just been formed within the American Federation of Labor (AFL), the United Auto Workers (UAW), and the radical socialist politics of the Popular Front (a broad coalition of different political groupings, often made up of centrists and leftists). The collaboration was an example of how modernism was viewed by some as a means to control cultural production by political action and thus prevent its dominance by business interests and capitalism. The idea of democratizing American culture was attractive to many elites of the time, who resented the new lucrative art form of industrial design being controlled by big business and, by the way, a handful of industrial design celebrities getting rich in the process. The school organizers recruited well-known industrial designers such as Raymond Loewy, Walter Dorwin Teague, Donald Deskey, George Sakier, and Russel Wright to lend legitimacy to an advisory board by the use of their names, and lured furniture designer Gilbert Rohde to serve as director. It is likely that these men were attracted by the idealistic intention of training young designers, as stated in the brochure, and as desperately needed in the field, rather than by the hidden leftist ideology behind it. This ideology was obvious in major study texts required by the curriculum, including The Theory of the Leisure Class, by Thorstein Veblen, published in 1899, which claimed that reform of industrial art had to be through the technology of machines, and highly influential sociologist/historian Lewis Mumford's book, Technics and Civilization (1934). Mumford considered streamlining as mere expressive and “outmoded decoration” and insisted that functionalist esthetics (such as the geometric abstract forms at the Bauhaus) was more appropriate for society, a view shared and promulgated by the Museum of Modern Art (MoMA), which venerated Bauhaus architects, and which would enthusiastically take up the cause of functional esthetics. The recent closure of the leftist Bauhaus by the Nazis as “a Marxist manifestation” generated much political sympathy throughout the liberal and progressive art community, which actively sought to bring exiled Bauhaus refugees to the United States to lead the modern movement they had initiated in Europe. Support of the Bauhaus was seen by some as a gesture of patriotic defiance against Nazism and war. Others saw communism as a similar deterrent to the evils of fascism, since Hitler's government was fervently anti-communist, and communists were fighting incessantly against Hitler and fascism. Despite internal political and philosophical differences of faculty members, the school implemented a provisional three-year design curriculum in August 1936, which promised to “present the ... standards of taste and style ... in the so-called International Style"” (the term invented and promoted by MoMA starting in 1932 and based on the Bauhaus). At the same time, an essay by the Federal Art Project (FAP) historian published in The Magazine of Art in October 1936 denigrated the “handful of super-men who are revolutionizing everything from hairpins to locomotives at a fabulous price," an unmistakable reference to the leading industrial designers. Many students and faculty of the Design Laboratory joined militant organizations of cultural workers, including those where communists, socialists, or Trotskyites (those supporting the political and economic theories of communism advocated by Leon Trotsky and his followers, usually including the principle of worldwide revolution) predominated, and instigated strikes and demonstrations demanding autonomy of the school, calling for a “United Front against War and Fascism," all of which put them at odds with FAP administrators. In December, several students and faculty were arrested for public protests and demonstrations.' Despite the labor unrest and strikes, 1936 was dawning with signs of economic recovery. The main economic indicators had regained the levels of the late 1920s, except for unemployment, still high at 11 percent, though this was much lower than the 25 percent of 1933. Industrial production had recovered significantly, and many businesses were getting back to normal, thus increasing design activity. In 1936, Montgomery Ward volume rose from 25 percent to 82 percent over Sears.2 By then, Anne Swainson was supervising 30 designers at Montgomery Ward's Bureau of Design, many of whom went on to become prominent industrial designers. Among the first was Dave Chapman, who had served as head of the Bureau's product design department. Chapman opened his own office in Chicago in 1936, which would become the largest industrial design firm in Chicago. Chapman had married his wife, Eileen, in about 1935. In New York City, mayor Fiorello La Guardia had appointed Norman Bel Geddes to the Municipal Arts Committee of New York in 1935,4 and Walter Dorwin Teague was appointed as one of the eight directors on the New York World Fair's Board of Design in 1936, as planning began for the 1939 event. In 1935, Bel Geddes designed and produced Dead End in New York. He toured the city in police cars for atmosphere and recorded river and street sounds for four days for use in the play's background. He had hired Robert Gruen (1913–1999) that year as a set designer, and Gruen would open his own glassware design office in New York in 1940. Also in 1935, Egmont Arens established his own design firm in New York, promoting what he called "industrial humaneering," emphasizing simplicity and function. John M. Little of Minneapolis joined Van Doren & Associates in Toledo in 1936. Little knew Van Doren from Minneapolis, where Van Doren had been assistant director of the Minneapolis Institute of Arts (MIA) until 1930, and Little had opened an industrial design office there in 1931. In 1937 Donald Dailey (1914-1997) would join Van Doren's firm as design director. Dailey was also from Minneapolis and had attended art classes at the MIA. He had also studied marketing and engineering at the University of Toledo (1934–1936) and design at the Toledo Museum School of Design (1936–1937). Henry Dreyfuss had been retained in 1934 by the Hoover Company to design vacuum cleaners. His annual retainer fee was \$25,000 (\$435,000 in 2013 dollars), and his first design, the Hoover Model 150 upright vacuum cleaner, debuted in 1936, beginning Dreyfuss's association with Hoover that would last for 20 years. That year, he also designed a typewriter for Royal, accessories for United Airlines DC-3s, and his first streamlined Mercury K-5 Pacific train design for the New York Central Railroad, marketed as the “Train of Tomorrow." For the locomotive, Dreyfuss covered the exterior pipes, whistles, and fittings with a smooth, “bathtub” cowl, with cutaway sides to display the driving wheels and rods, which were dramatized at night by concealed spotlights under the cowling on either side. Dreyfuss also designed the interiors on the train, including the coaches, kitchen, dining car, smoking lounge, parlor car, and observation car, all with arrangements designed to avoid the traditional “linear" look of railroad coaches and to have the atmosphere of a private club. For example, the dining car was in three sections, two with conventional seating, but the middle section was made up of tables for two seated side-by-side, facing the center aisle. In the rounded-end observation car, Dreyfuss lowered the windowsills to maximize the view through four-foot-high windows. Instead of having seating arranged around the walls, facing in, he placed the seating in the center, facing out toward the windows. Later versions of the Mercury included the Chicago-Detroit Mercury and the Chicago Cincinnati Mercury. The first Cleveland-Detroit Mercury would last run in 1959. Dreyfuss was not the only train designer. In February 1936, Raymond Loewy, with employee Carl Otto (b. ca. 1910–ca. 1986 ), hired in 1935, designed the Pennsylvania Railroad's K-4s streamlined shroud for steam locomotive No. 3768, hiding most of the mechanical details. Otto was born Carl Louis Otto in Michigan and was trained as an engineer at Michigan State University. He began his career as a draftsman with Duesenberg in Auburn, Indiana, and then went to New York to work for Norman Bel Geddes. Loewy did not follow the “bathtub” streamlining shrouds of previous trains, but decided to retain the unique character of the traditional, cylindrical boilers of steam locomotives. He basically designed a bullet nose for a cylindrical boiler and placed it upon a streamlined platform covering the driver wheels that suggested the top half of a bullet. He would refine the design into the S-1 in 1939. Loewy had also acquired the Studebaker account in 1935 and was retained for the design of exteriors of the 1937 models, at the same time as Helen Dryden, former art director of the Dura Company in Ohio, who had been retained at \$100,000 per year for Studebaker car interior designs since 1934, worked on the interior. At the time, she was acclaimed as “the highest-paid woman artist." Loewy's exterior design, completed in 1936, featured a narrow, vertical grille with horizontal louvers, the top three of which extended rearward along the side of the hood. Headlights were teardrop shaped. Dryden was publicly credited in Studebaker ads as “the stylist” for the 1937 Studebaker, but she was dropped as a consultant that same year. Loewy would continue to design Studebaker cars until the company went out of business in 1961. In 1935, Walter Dorwin Teague pioneered in the branding of gasoline service stations when he designed his first for Texaco, which included not just the logotype, but a functional, rain-protected architecture and layout that would become a standard benchmark for all service stations over the next two decades. In 1936, he also designed the Kodak Bantam Special camera in classic Art Deco style for his longtime client. That same year, Brooks Stevens designed a custom Zephyr "Land Yacht" for William Woods Plankinton, consisting of a streamlined truck cab and 17-foot hitchable trailer with an electric icebox, full kitchen with electric stove, bathroom with shower, living room, hunting and fishing equipment, and sleeping quarters for eight. The entire length of the rig was 45 feet, displaying a most modern and exotic mobile home for its well-to-do owner. Norman Bel Geddes in 1936 was busy designing exhibits for the 1939 World's Fair, a truck for the Autocar Company, and the first all-metal soda dispenser for the Walter Kidde Corporation, as well as exhibiting his designs at the Museum of Science and Industry in Chicago and designing stage sets for The Eternal Road in New York.6 In June 1936, Carnegie Tech graduated the first five students ever to receive a college degree in industrial design. When degrees were awarded alphabetically at graduation, the first to receive a degree was Maud (Bowers) Rice (1913–1998), a Philippine national. Donald Dohner was now at Pratt Institute in Brooklyn, starting a three-year program in industrial design, with the first graduates due in 1939. Peter Müller-Munk now headed the Art and Design Department at Carnegie. Alexander Kostellow, of the Carnegie industrial design faculty, and his wife, Rowena Reed, would depart to join Dohner at Pratt in 1938. Kostellow would soon become a prominent and influential faculty member in the Pratt program.7 In 1936, the Homer Laughlin China Company introduced a line of mass-produced ceramic tableware for the home called Fiestaware, designed by Frederick Hurten Rhead (1880– 1942). The brightly colored, mix-and-match, modern line was highly successful in the market and is still a classic design valued highly by collectors. Rhead had emigrated from the United Kingdom in 1902 and worked for several ceramics factories, including Roseville, where he was director from 1904 to 1908; Jervis Pottery on Long Island; and from 1917 to 1927, he directed research at American Encaustic Tiling Company in Zanesville, Ohio. From 1927 until his death, he would remain as art director for Homer Laughlin. In July 1936, the Spanish Civil War began, and in November, Franklin Roosevelt was elected by a landslide to his second term as president of the United States. Unemployment was still at 14 percent. Roosevelt would initiate payroll taxes to pay for Social Security, and with the threat of unrest in Europe, he would establish the Neutrality Act intended to avoid any involvement in taking sides in disputes between nations. In Chicago, Lawrence H. Whiting, director of the American Furniture Mart, invited a group of furniture designers to a luncheon at the Furniture Club to hear speakers Donald Deskey, Gilbert Rohde, Kem Weber, and Leo Jiranek, all mentioned earlier. Possibly influenced by the Wagner Act of 1935, which established federal rights of workers to organize unions, to engage in collective bargaining, and to take part in strikes, attendees at the 1936 meeting at the Furniture Club agreed to form a new organization: the Designers' Institute of the American Furniture Mart (DIAFM).8 This appeared to be intended as a union of furniture designers, since a main requirement of membership restricted member design activities to furniture design, a restriction that was not appreciated by many industrial designers, who were designing everything from locomotives to wash machines. As we noted earlier in the formation of the Design Laboratory, this was a time of intense labor unrest. From September 1936 to May 1937, half a million workers were involved in sit-down strikes nationally. A strike against Republic Steel resulted in four deaths and 84 injuries. By the spring of 1937, the WPA designated the Design Laboratory as one of the many programs to be discontinued, as the school struggled with layoffs, violence, and confusion. A radical, leftist, professional trade union, the Federation of Architects, Engineers, Chemists and Technicians (FAECT) came to the rescue as the new sponsor and to provide direction of the school. In the summer session, the curriculum was revamped along the lines of the Bauhaus Foundations Course and was turned into a four-year BA and BFA program comparable to standard universities. Enrollment increased to 400, and administrators hoped it would become a "labor university." Other private educational institutions of design were being established or initiated at the same time. The University of Illinois at Champaign-Urbana established a BFA program of industrial design in 1937. Cooper Union in New York offered a four-year certificate in plastic design in 1937, with instructors Paul Feeley and Lila Ulrich, a Bauhaus graduate. The California Graduate School of Design in Pasadena organized a two-year graduate program in industrial design with advice from Richard F. Bach of the Metropolitan Museum of Art, Royal Bailey Farnum of the Rhode Island School of Design, Peter Müller-Munk of the Carnegie Institute ofTechnology, and architect George Howe. It was the only graduate program of industrial design in the country. Graduate engineers were taught the principles of product engineering and industrial design. Architect Hunt Lewis was named initial director of the school, and he invited as faculty members Walter P. Baermann (1903–1972), a 1932 émigré from Munich who had served as educational director of the Springfield (Massachusetts) Art Museum; Karl With (1891–1980), a German art historian and educator who had fled the Nazi regime in 1933; and Kem Weber, who also taught at the Art Center School of Design since 1932, and who had recently designed his famous 1934 Airline chair. Baermann was soon appointed director, and he remained in that position until 1941, when the school was merged into the graduate engineering curriculum of the California Institute of Technology (CalTech) and renamed the California School of Design.9 At the Cranbrook Academy of Art near Detroit, Charles Eames (1907–1978) joined the faculty in 1937 and headed the department of experimental design to explore new materials such as plastics and plywood for furniture. Eames was born in St. Louis and studied architecture at Washington University from 1924 to 1926. He worked with an architectural firm in St. Louis from 1925 to 1927, and then as a partner in the firm Gray & Eames from 1930 to 1937. At Cranbrook, Eero Saarinen (1910–1961), the son of Cranbrook's founder, Finnish architect Eliel Saarinen (1873–1950), had graduated from Yale in architecture in 1934 and joined his father in 1935. In 1937, Eero began working with Eames, along with Italian émigré Harry Bertoia (1915–1978). With the expansion of industrial design education and the intense publicity generated by the new profession, men and women were entering the field in scores. Counting the automotive, railroad, airline, appliance, and furniture industries, the population of industrial designers was probably in the hundreds by this time. The purpose of this book is to focus on those pioneers who were active in the profession before 1935, so while important designers from this point on will be mentioned, their biographies and careers will be minimal, and the text will focus on those earliest dozens of machine designers that are of specific interest in this book. German refugees from the Bauhaus were flocking to America. Bauhaus master Josef Albers (1888–1976) and his wife, Anni (1899–1994), had arrived in 1933 to become faculty members at Black Mountain College in North Carolina. Walter Gropius, former director of the Bauhaus, who had opened an office in London after his resignation from the Bauhaus in 1928, arrived in the United States in 1937 to become a professor in the graduate program at the Harvard School of Architecture, along with his Bauhaus student, Marcel Breuer (1902– 1981). Làszló Moholy-Nagy, also of the Bauhaus but in exile in London at the time, became, upon the recommendation of Gropius, director of the School of Design at the Association of Arts and Industries in Chicago, which he opened in 1937 and promptly renamed the New Bauhaus: American School of Design. Hin Bredendieck (1904-1995), a former Bauhaus student; Georgy Kepes (1906–2001), a Hungarian designer who had worked for Moholy-Nagy in his Berlin studio; and Alexander Archipenko (1887-1964), a Russian artist and sculptor who had worked in Berlin and immigrated to the United States in 1929, all joined Moholy-Nagy at the school. The school attracted only 20 students and would close in 1938 under financial difficulties, but it would be reopened in 1939 as the School of Design, under the patronage of Walter Paepcke, president of the Container Corporation of America. Many industrial designers were enjoying a busy year in 1937. Detroit industrial design consultant George Walker began designing Nash automobiles, a client he would work for until 1945. Brooks Stevens designed a Model B tractor for Allis-Chalmers, which was lighter and cheaper than its key competitor, Ford's Farmall, and a streamlined sales van for the Western Printing and Lithograph Company based on his 1936 Zephyr land yacht design. Henry Dreyfuss designed a whitewall Double Eagle automotive tire for Goodyear. Bell Labs introduced the “Phone of the Future” that Dreyfuss had been working on since 1930— a black phenolic plastic 300 tabletop phone that combined the transmitter and receiver into a single combined handset resting in a horizontal cradle. It replaced the “candlestick” model that had been introduced in 1916, and which, in 1919, had a rotary dial added. The classic 300 would be produced until 1950. Otto Kuhler, working with the Baltimore & Ohio (B&O) Railroad as art director of the BO magazine, designed its logo and its 1937 streamlined, bullet-nosed, steam locomotives, known as the “Kuhler type," for B&O's Royal Blue train on the Washington to New York run. The locomotive, a 4–6–2 “Pacific" Class, was painted light gray and royal blue. Time magazine reported it as “swashbuckling” and as “symbolizing the new era in railroading." The design was virtually copied by Raymond Loewy for his 1939 S-1 locomotive. Unfortunately, the Royal Blue line only ran as far as Jersey City, and passengers had to be transported to Manhattan by White Motor Company buses, which Kuhler also designed. Despite all the streamlined shrouds designed for steam locomotives, the days of steam engines were numbered. Diesel power would replace it. In May 1937, as opening ceremonies for San Francisco's Golden Gate Bridge transpired, the flagship of the Acheson, Topeka, & Santa Fe Railway, the Super Chief, began regular service between Chicago and Los Angeles. Departing Los Angeles at 7:30 a.m., it reached Chicago in 36 hours and 49 minutes, setting a record by averaging 60 mph, often reaching 100 mph. Called the “Train of the Stars" because of the many celebrities from Hollywood it carried, it had been under development since 1935, when General Motors' Electro-Motive Corporation (EMC) delivered two diesel-electric units, not much more than boxes on wheels. Just before regular service was initiated, the heavyweight standard cars were replaced with lightweight stainless-steel cars built by the Budd Company in Philadelphia, including a dome-topped observation deck in one. The streamlined engine was distinguished by its raised, forward-facing operating cab, with split windows similar to an automobile or airliner. This was not unusual, considering that the General Motors Art and Colour Section department under Harley Earl executed the styling. A dramatic “warbonnet" color scheme of a red nose and cab with a bright yellow cartouche with Santa Fe logo was styled by Leland Knickerbocker from the GM Art and Colour Section. Philadelphia architects Paul Cret and his partner, John Harbeson, designed the Budd Company car interiors. The Super Chief would operate until Amtrak would take over the nation's passenger service in May 1971. Raymond Loewy in 1937 considered himself an expert in locomotive design, and at the height of his involvement with the Pennsylvania Railroad, he published a 7.5 × 10 inch book with 125 illustrations, The New Vision Locomotive, showing the aesthetics of locomotive evolution, and including photos of his K-4 design as it was evolving into the S-1. The long-term success of many product designs of the 1930s seems to conflict with the concept described in 1932 by Elmo Calkins of “planned obsolescence," which predicted that annual design changes spur consumer spending. But the longevity of certain product designs can be explained by several reasons. First, while many consumers are eager to obtain newer versions of a product, there are also some who are not and who will keep and use a favored product until it expires mechanically. Second, many new, industrial-designed products, for the first time in the 1930s, acquired a shape, style, and configuration that seemed "right" for its assigned task, establishing what is called a “typeform," that is, a form that seemed to many the ideal for a particular type, or category, of product. Other manufacturing competitors, noting the popular success of a particular brand, would imitate the design, therefore increasing public familiarity and acceptance of the standard typeform until some substantially different, more popular, configuration replaced it as the new typeform for that particular product. The KitchenAid mixer survives because it is regarded as the ideal typeform for this particular category of products. The power of historically successful designs is illustrated by the automotive industry, which has had dozens of different, successive typeforms over the years, but would remain nostalgic about car designs of the 1930s, which produced the first designs that were truly modern. Detroit in the late 1990s and early 2000s would develop a number of so-called retro designs, intending to recall that period, and which were intended to replicate the original typeforms. They included the (new) Volkswagen Beetle (1994), the Plymouth Prowler (1994), Chrysler's PT (Personal Transportation) Cruiser (1999), Buick's 2001 Blackhawk, Ford's 2002 Forty-Nine, and Chevrolet's HHR (Heritage High Roof) in 2006, all in 1930s styles. But in the late 1930s, the problem was too many cars, and not enough roads. Since half of all households now owned an automobile, the U.S. road system was being challenged by an increasing number of cars on narrow, crowded roads. It was an obvious problem addressed by Norman Bel Geddes, on assignment for the J. Walter Thompson advertising agency in 1937. Based on his experience with his scale-model racetrack of the 1920s, and his war game of the early 1930s, he designed a model City of Tomorrow with expressways featured in a national advertising campaign for the Shell Oil Company. The model would be expanded to become the Futurama exhibit for General Motors at the 1939 New York World's Fair, which in turn would inspire the future federal interstate highway system. Jumping rom highway systems to cocktail parties, Bel Geddes also designed a Skyscraper cocktail shaker and a Manhattan serving tray in 1937. The automobile industry was transformed during the Depression. Car production fell from 5.6 million in 1929 to 1.4 million in 1932. Dozens of auto manufacturing brands in 1929 were reduced to 10 in 1935, which included the Big Three of Ford, Chrysler, and General Motors. GM, with five new models since 1930, led the pack and in 1936 was unionized. In 1937, GM's Art and Colour Section would change its name to the Styling Division. Harley Earl had a staff of 200 designers, cranking out annual model changes on the fleet of GM cars. Earl supervised five studios, one for each division (Cadillac, Buick, Oldsmobile, Pontiac, and Chevrolet), as well as 12 special studios. Yet he wanted more designers, and so GM and its Fisher Body Division initiated a talent search by sponsoring and promoting a national, 1:12 scale model (one inch equals one foot) “Dream Car” design competition that awarded college scholarships to the best original student designs for a four-door sedan (later various different body styles were added). It was called the Fisher Body Craftsman's Guild, which would continue for 30 more years, encouraging a new generation of automotive designers, many of whom would end up in Detroit, and not a few in Earl's studios. Actually, the contest was a major addition to an earlier Fisher Body Craftsman's Guild contest that was initiated in 1930, not to innovate designs, but to accurately replicate Fisher plans for a miniature scale model (1:15 to 1:18, where one inch equaled 1.25 to 1.6 feet) of a 19th-century horse-drawn Napoleonic coach, the trademark adopted by Fisher in 1923, and which appeared on all production Fisher bodies made for GM. There were two competitive categories: the Junior Division (ages 12 through 15) and the Senior Division (ages 16 through 19). Winners won prize money for a scholarship to a college of their choice: \$5,000 for each of the top two winners in each category. That was more than double what a man typically earned in a year! The Napoleonic coach contest would continue along with the new model car competition until 1940, so applicants could choose either one to enter, but the Napoleonic contest ended at that time. Students would no longer be interested in historical craftsmanship, but in futuristic cars. In 1935, Amos Northup, automotive designer for the Murray Corporation of America, the independent Detroit auto body manufacturer, had patented his famous “shark-nose” hood design, which generally resembled its namesake. The design would be copied by most manufacturers in the late 1930s, including the 1938 Graham, the 1937 Willys “windswept” design, the 1936 Lincoln Zephyr, and the 1940 Studebaker, Chrysler, Plymouth, and Ford. But in 1937, Amos Northup died at age 48 as a result of slipping on ice and fatally cracking his skull. His designs were a powerful influence as cars in the 1930s were transformed from boxes to the sleek forms that are familiar to us today. Most GM production designs during the 1930s were not futuristic, dramatic or innovative, but followed the relatively conservative annual advance of styling. This changed in 1938, when Earl developed a more radical prototype Buick called only the Y-Job. Like the similarly sounding name iPhone by Apple in 2007 that predicted the future appearance of all handheld computer devices, the Y-Job predicted the shape and style of most postwar cars. Why the name "Y-Job"? Most experimental cars were tagged with an "X," sometimes with a name or number. Just to be different, Earl decided to use the letter “Y," the next letter in the alphabet. The Y-Job was designed by Oldsmobile studio head George Snyder under Earl's direct supervision, with highly innovative styling features of hidden headlights, suitcase fenders, low horizontal grille, flush push-button door handles, electric windows, a poweroperated electric convertible top, and a rounded fuselage body without traditional running boards. Most unusual was the Y-Job's low, long silhouette with a 58-inch (less than five feet) height at the top of the split, angled windshield, A dozen closely-spaced, parallel and horizontal chrome strips (speed lines) adorned the lower half of both front and rear fenders, the chrome bumpers wrapped around the body corners, and a chrome “bombsight" hood ornament, which would become a Buick trademark, was the icing on the cake. The Y-Job was never shown to the public until after the war, but Earl used it as his personal transportation and it attracted a lot of public attention that way. Some of its features would appear in the 1938 Cadillac 60 Special, designed by Bill Mitchell (1912–1988), who had been hired by Earl in 1935. But after the war, the 1950 Buick would look almost like a clone of the 1938 Y-Job. In 1938, Earl became a vice president of GM, and he would rule GM styling for the next 20 years. In 2001, Buick would recreate the Y-Job with modern advancements called the Buick Blackhawk, drawing extensively from the Y-Job and the 1939 Buick. It was entirely handmade, cost \$475,000, and would be featured in the 2003 ilm Bad Boys II. On June 15, 1938, Dreyfuss saw the introduction of his design oflocomotive shrouds for the New York Central Railroad's 20th Century Limited, which was a refinement of his Mercury K-5 design of the previous year. The shrouds hid the assembly of boiler valves, pipes, and operating parts of ten new J-3, 4–6–4 Hudson-type steam locomotives. The shrouds, similar to Loewy's K-4 for the Pennsylvania Railroad, featured bullet-nosed boiler shapes, but with a center fin that resembled ancient warrior helmets. Dreyfuss also designed the cars using blue, gray, and silver, and like the K-5, they were designed with asymmetric furnishings to avoid the tunnel look of most train interiors. Probably the most famous American train, the 20th Century Limited was an express train that operated from 1902 to 1967 between Grand Central Station in New York and LaSalle Street Station in Chicago. The new train by Dreyfuss made the trip in 16 hours. That same year, Dreyfuss also designed Model A and Model B tractors for John Deere, a new client he had acquired in 1937. Raymond Loewy was having a banner year in 1938, the year he became an American citizen. He opened a branch office in South Bend, Indiana, to service his major client, Studebaker. To head the group, he appointed Virgil Exner (1909–1973), formerly with Harley Earl at General Motors, who had joined Loewy in 1937. Exner, born in Ann Arbor, Michigan, studied art at Notre Dame but had to drop out in 1928 due to lack of funds. He took a job as an advertising artist at an agency handling the Studebaker account. He was hired by Earl in 1933, where he became styling chief for Pontiac and designed Pontiac's famous “Silver Streak" hood ornament. ![](images/a57674d97c327a6acd73fd389c7f7d2c8310e9b802c3f29a30a7635828452133.jpg) Pennsylvania Railroad's 20th Century Limited, by Henry Dreyfuss. Loewy also hired Clare Hodgman, who had started his career in the early 1930s with General Motors in the Oldsmobile studio, and who had worked for Loewy on the Sears Coldspot refrigerator project in 1932–1935. The first task of the group was to begin work on the 1939 Studebaker Champion, a low-cost model. They also designed a new Studebaker “Big S” logo and emblem. Other Loewy designs that year included a Metro series of light- and medium-duty trucks and vans for the International Harvester Company. ![](images/fb3eb1bbd87756b5c78c7421a45fc85bcefd76c1226718b35bcb68b919b6eb94.jpg) Virgil Exner, ca. 1953. Loewy had established a personal office in London in 1935, but now in 1938, he staffed it to function on a continuous basis, with Carl Otto (d. 1986) as head, along with Claire E. Hodgman. The new expanded office designed a logo for the British Petroleum (BP) Company, while the New York office designed the main hall for the ocean liner Panama. Hodgman worked on the Hillman Minx and the Rootes Sunbeam Talbot, and also designed conceptual racecars for the British Autocar magazine of March 1938. One of Otto's accounts was Standard Auto (Triumph). In 1938, Brooks Stevens designed the first commercially successful steam iron for the Edmilton Company in Milwaukee, the Steam-O-Matic, which was introduced at Macy's in New York and Marshall Field's in Chicago. Viktor Schreckengost, head of the Cleveland School (now Institute) of Art, began creating stage and costume designs for Cleveland theater productions and continued to produce award-winning ceramic sculptures for Cleveland Museum of Art shows. Kem Weber was designing a line of tubular steel chairs for the Lloyd Manufacturing Company in Menominee, Michigan. In Chicago, Ann Swainson, head of Montgomery Ward's Bureau of Design, designed a plastic radio cabinet, and her staff designed special china called Cascade, which was exhibited at the large-scale exhibition on modern design at the Metropolitan Museum of Art, and which became one of Montgomery Ward's most popular patterns. Swainson had traveled in 1937 to her native Sweden, visiting factories, department stores, and art schools, and was impressed with the Swedish government's part-time training of industrial designers in schools and factories. Elsewhere in the United States, other corporations were hiring and engaging industrial designers as part of their merchandizing strategy, seeking increased sales and modern style. In 1938, a major corporation was now expanding its industrial design activity into all visual aspects of corporate identity. Libbey-Owens-Ford (LOF) was formed in 1931 by the merger of Libbey-Owens Sheet Glass Company and another Toledo company, Edward Ford Plate Glass Company, which pioneered in automotive safety glass. In 1937, H. Creston Doner had been invited by LOF to leave his post with Goodrich in Akron, Ohio, to come to Toledo to direct the department of design there starting in 1938, a position he would hold until 1967. He supervised the architectural and interior design operations, as well as promotional campaigns for color and lighting effects; the design of LOF warehouses, showrooms, offices, and retail stores; and the design of a new corporate image for truck fleets, building signs, business forms, and advertising materials. Meanwhile, in 1935, Libbey Glass Manufacturing Company in Toledo had been acquired by Owens-Illinois Glass Company and became a subsidiary. In 1938, Edwin W. Fuerst was appointed to head the design department at Libbey Glass, while his brother, Dwight Fuerst headed the design area for bottling and packaging at Owens-Illinois. Edwin designed Greenbrier pattern stemware for Libbey. ![](images/cf40672fc07c38c29f183e5afbf9a10e24a8af9411cf60d600d088e109f78e43.jpg) Philco refrigerator, 1938, by Harold Van Doren (Patent No. D131,103 U.S. Patent Office). Elsewhere in Toledo, Harold Van Doren & Associates were also quite busy. Van Doren himself had worked as a color consultant to Libbey-Owens-Ford's Vitrolite division in 1937. But in 1938, Van Doren's office was now designing refrigerators for Philco, a Philadelphia company, and personal weighing scales and the Guardian Duplex Scale for the Toledo Scale Company. Another industrial designer, Walter Dorwin Teague, also had clients in Toledo in 1938. He had been called in as a consultant to the Owens-Illinois Glass Company, which had acquired the Libbey Glass Company. Teague was to work with two divisions: bottling and packaging, and structural. He designed an Insulis glass construction block in a Circon pattern for the Owens-Illinois Glass Company's structural division, which then asked him to design the division's offices in the Chrysler Building in New York. Additional design organizations began to be formed. In Chicago in July 1938, the Designer's Institute of the American Furniture Mart (DIAFM), founded in 1936 under the auspices of Lawrence H. Whiting, director of the American Furniture Mart, met for the first time since its founding, “at the instigation of Whiting, Alfred Auerbach, and Richard F. Bach of the Metropolitan Museum of Art."1º The principal speaker this time was John Vassos, then head of design at the Radio Corporation of America (RCA) in New York. In his address, Vassos urged that the organization divorce itself from the patronage and support of the Furniture Mart, because it restricted member work to furniture design. In fact, most members were furniture designers, and Vassos himself designed radios, which were often enclosed in wooden furniture. Vassos suggested the organization extend its practices beyond that of furniture design into consumer products, packaging, automotive design or any number of additional design opportunities then available. The group, comprised of 45 cofounding members, agreed with him. Among them were certainly Alfons Bach, Leo Jiranek, Al Lambach, Ernie Swarts, Belle Kogan, and probably, although not confirmed, Lionel C. Algoren (1896–1984), Kem Weber, Donald Deskey, and Gilbert Rohde. They changed the name of the organization on the spot to the American Designers' Institute (ADI) to indicate that it was a national, not just a Chicago organization. Even though Vassos was not currently a member of the group before his presentation, the group elected him the first president of ADI, which would become incorporated in the state of Illinois in 1940. Belle Kogan was elected secretary and treasurer, and she set up meetings, often with speakers, keeping the minutes and other records. Her only help came from her own office staff. ADI also established a chapter in New York." ![](images/f8d1046e18c40a4d7d40c9783f38c449f50f48af48cb97785f507ee1b26065d4.jpg) ADI logo, 1938.
