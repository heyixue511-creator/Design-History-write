# Multinational Corporations and Global Products ## The corporate personality: invention and identity From a critical perspective it may be argued that the idea of corporate identity or personality, as articulated by designers and consultancies, is often little more than a contradiction in terms [97, 98].

As the American economist J.
K.
Galbraith argued in his introduction to the 1974 British edition of his widely-read book, The New Industrial State: It is central to my case that power in modern industrial society resides with the large producing organisations—the corporations.
So, far from being safely and resignedly subordinate to the market, as the neo-classical argument holds, they fix prices, and go on extensively to accommodate the consumer to their needs.
And they also obtain from the state such further action as is needed to ensure a benign and stable environment for their operations.9 This, Galbraith argued, was brought about through the economic power of the large-scale corporation to compete with the state in terms of planning and influence.
In 1968, General Motors had a gross revenue of \$22.8 billion, equivalent to about one-eighth of the total receipts of Federal Government in the United States.
It accorded with Galbraith's model of large-scale American capitalism in its ability, through sheer size, to influence government, control markets, and dictate prices and wage settlements for the whole automobile industry (only one element of its industrial activities), as well as establishing patterns of consumer taste.

In Britain, for example, the great railway companies of the nineteenth century made concerted attempts to project their own individual identities across many aspects of their visible presence, from railway termini through to coach livery, uniforms, and dining-car tableware.
Design historians have laid particular emphasis on the corporate identity work of designers such as Peter Behrens at the Allgemeine Elektrizitäts-Gesellschaft in the early years of the twentieth century in Germany, as discussed in Chapter 1.
Significant examples of corporate identity policies can be found in many other countries, as in Britain in the buildings, locomotives, rolling stock, buses, signage, advertising, fittings, and fabrics of the London Passenger Transport Board under the operationaldirection of Frank Pick in the 193os, or in the United States withWalter Dorwin Teague's striking designs for Texaco petrol stations.
Other notable examples prior to the Second World War include the Olivetti office equipment company in Italy, where a corporate identity policy emerged in the wake of Adriano Olivetti's initiatives in modernizing the firm's business and production methods in the later 192os.
Much of the impetus had derived from a visit to the United States in 1926, and an advertising office was established in 1928.
Following his appointment as general manager in 1933, a wide range of initiatives were adopted in the projection of the public, and also internal, identity of the company, sustained by the employment of a number of leading modernist architects and designers who worked on all aspects of the company's visible appearance, from brochures, advertising andoffice machines through to factory buildings and workers' housing.
A Bauhaus graduate, Alexander Schawinsky, workedon graphic and product design from 1933 to 1936; Marcello Nizzoli, a renowned exhibition and graphic designer, became the company's chief consultant in 1936; in the same year Giovanni Pintori also joined the company and, like Nizzoli, added to Olivetti's reputation through publicity, product, and architectural design.
This very much set the scene for the further development of an enlightened corporate policy in the post-Second World War era withthe employment of designers of the stature of Ettore Sottsass Jr. and Mario Bellini and the increasingly significant role given to cultural projection.
The company's commitment to corporate identity design was recognized in 1974 when the American Institute of Architects awarded Olivetti the Industrial Arts Medal for a history of excellence in communicating its image through product design, corporate communications, architecturally distinguished manufacturing and merchandising facilities, and sponsorship of numerous social, educational, recreational, and cultural programs for both its employees and the public at large'.
In the United States corporate identity design also assumed increasing importance in the interwar years.
The Container Corporation of America (CCA) was an important pioneer in this respect in the 193os, largely through the enlightened patronage of the company's founder,the Chicago industrialistWalter P.
Paepke.
In 1936, wishing to communicate the company's commitment to a modern outlook and product quality, he employed the N.
W.
Ayer advertising agency as a means of bringing this about.
In the same year he also appointed Egbert Jacobsen as Director of the company's new Design Department.
Amongst those designers commissioned to produce advertisements were the French designers A.
M.
Cassandre and Fernand Léger, the former Bauhaus tutors Lázló Moholy-Nagy and Herbert Bayer, Herbert Matter, the Swiss emigré designer and poster artist, and the Hungarian-born graphic designer, Gyorgy Kepes.
Combinations of photomontage with typography and other techniques associated with the European avant-garde were incorporated into company publicity, strongly influencing the outlook of other designers working for the company.
Such strong links with European modernism were sustained by the establishment, in 1937, of the New Bauhaus in Chicago under Lázló Moholy-Nagy.
Later becoming the School of Design, then School of Design inChicago, and finally the Institute of Design,15 a number of former Bauhaus graduates and masters were employed on the staff.
In the post-Second World War period Walter Paepke, Egbert Jacobsen, and Herbert Bayer were at the forefront of moves which sought to further understanding in business circles of the significant economic potential of design in industry.
In 1951 Paepke inaugurated the first Design Conference at the Aspen Institute of Humanistic Studies in Colorado.16Initiating debate about the wider social, cultural, and philosophical implications of design it sought to bring together leading figures from design, industry, government, and education.
At the 1951 meetingboth Olivetti and the Container Corporation of America were cited asexemplars of the implementation of a consistent design policy in industry.
Held annually, in 1954 it became incorporated as the International Design Conference in Aspen (IDCA) and was soon established as a major forum for design debate, with contributions from leading designers, intellectuals, and industrialists from all over the world. ## Design and the multinational corporation after 1945 As has been discussed in Chapter 4, the aspirations of those seeking to implement the modernist creed in the interwar years had been severely blunted by the rising tide of national identity in the increasingly difficult political and economic circumstances of the times.
However, after the Second World War, with the widespread discrediting of totalitarian regimes and the waning of the power of imperialism in the Western industrialized world, the internationalizing values inherent in modernism no longer carried the same threat to national identity.
With the postwar reorientation of traditional trading patterns and the need to develop new outlets many large-scale companies viewed the penetration of global markets as an increasingly important goal.
As part of this strategy multinational corporations assumed the mantle of modernism, their international presence marked by efficient-looking buildings, products, and co-ordinated corporate design programmes.
As has been suggested earlier, the Olivetti company epitomized high standards for corporate identity design and implementation.
It was particularly influential on the outlook of International Business Machines (IBM), a company which emerged as a world leader in the field of office equipment after the end of the Second World War.
IBM's distinctive corporate style, which embraced everything from brochures and sign systems to dictating machines and company buildings, emerged after the appointment of the architect-designer Eliot Noyes as Consultant Director of Design in 1956 [104].
He had first worked on the IBM account in Norman Bel Geddes's office ten years earlier, continuing to work on a company retainer after setting up his own design practice in 1947.
He brought in Paul Rand as Graphics Coordinator as well as a number of other design consultants including Charles Eames, George Nelson, and Edgar Kaufmann Jr.

There was a similar institutionalizationof the notion of Good Design in Europe, often with support from the State, as with the Council of Industrial Design (COID), established in Britain in 1944 or the Rat für Formgebung (Design Council) in Germany, which, following a 1951 mandate in the German Parliament, was inaugurated in 1953.
The COID was charged from the outset with ‘improving' standards of design in the consumer and manufacturing sectors and initiated a Good Design Award Scheme for selected products in 1957, the year after opening its own Design Centre in the Haymarket, London.
The awardsgenerally recognized products which conformed to the modernist canon—-genteel and restrained, and devoid of excessive ornamentation or ephemeral styling.
Similar awards reflecting parallel aesthetic priorities were offered in other European countries, including the Compasso d'Oro scheme, initiated by La Rinascente department store in Italy in 1954, and the Beauté France award, made through the French Ministry of Commerce.
That there was an established international canon of taste' in the 1950s can be argued further through the evidence collected by Jay Doblin, Director of the Institute of Design at the Illinois Institute of Technology, in 1958.25 He wrote to 10o of the leading' designers, architects, and design teachers around the world, asking them to identify the ten 'best designed mass-produced products of modern times'.
About 8o ballots were returned and, after the issue of further questionnaires, a final order was drawn up.
Although the overall ranking was significantly more accommodating with regard to ephemeral styling than would have been the case with MOMA, theCOID, and comparable organizations with a commitment to the concept of Good Design, the top three products were the Olivetti Lettera 22 portable typewriter of 195o, designed by Marcello Nizzoli, the Eames side-chair of 1947, produced by Herman Miller, and Mies Van Der Rohe's Barcelona Chair, originally designed in 1929, produced by Knoll Associates.

Cassina, the Milan-based furniture company, played a similar role, movinginto the field of architect-designed furniture from the late 194os onwards, with a particular emphasis on Italian designers, such as Franco Albini, Gio Ponti, Vico Magistretti, and Mario Bellini.
From 1965 it also initiated a policy of producing a range of past classics from the late nineteenth and earlier twentieth centuries.
Entitled the Masters'series, it commenced with manufacture, to the original design, of the 1929 chaise longue by Le Corbusier, Charlotte Perriand, and Pierre Jeanneret.
Later, particularly in the heritage-aware 198os when thepervasive fashionabilityof Post-Modernism cast many aspects of modernist design into a historical perspective, the Masters' range included the resurrection of furniture by Gunnar Asplund, Breuer, Frank Lloyd Wright, Gerrit Rietveld, and CharlesRennie Mackintosh.
Also important in the wider projection of companies has been their involvement in cultural activities, a sphere in which Olivetti has been particularly prominent.
Exhibitions specifically centred upon the company's own products and designers were widely disseminated, typified by such shows as Olivetti: Form and Concept put on at the Musée des Arts Decoratifs in Paris (as well as at venues in Barcelona, Madrid, Edinburgh, London, and Tokyo between 1969 and 1971) and Os Artistas e a Olivetti (The Artists and Olivetti) shown in Sao Paolo, Brazil in 1976.
However, wider aspects of Italian artistic heritage were celebrated in a series of art-historical films by Carlo Ludovico Ragghianti in the 195os, as well as in exhibitions such as the Italian Modern Art Exhibition 191o-35, drawn from the Mattioli Collection, which was shown in New York and Europe in the late 196os and early 197os.
Film andphotography have also been a potentvehicle for the projection of company ethos, environment, design practice, and social policy, with commissions from, and about, other countries, cultures, ecology, and the environment.
Even gifts, souvenirs, desk diaries, and company calendars, first introduced in 1951, were a part of the process of establishing the company's personality as cultural.
Corporate gifts and souvenirs have included melamine paper knives by Marcello Nizzoli (196o), a melamine desk tidy by Bruno Munari (196o), a canvas brief case by Giorgio Soavi (1965), and an enamelled-iron pencil tray (1966) and electric table clock (1968) by EttoreSottsass Jr., many of which became extremely sought after as status symbols by business executives and managers. ## 'The New International Style' Globalization of the designed object accelerated significantly in the 198os with the emergence of design groups such as Memphis.
It originated in 1981 in Milan and, centred around Ettore Sottsass Jr., effectively set on a commercial footingmany ideas which hadoriginated in the studio-based experimentation of 197os Italy.
Memphis furniture, lighting, ceramic, glass, and jewellery designs drew upon the symbols, forms, materials, and decorative motifs of mass culture; it utilized seemingly mundane materials such as plastic laminates, and emphasized surface decoration, colour, and layers of meaning at the expense of apparent function.
Such products radically undermined both the architectonic principles of the modernist aesthetic inherent in international style furniture and the linear gracefulness that had generally characterized design-conscious Italian products since the Second World War.
In addition to Sottsass, the group had a strong international range of contributors to its output, including Andrea Branzi, Michele De Lucchi, Matheo Thun, and Marco Zanini from Italy, Michael Graves and Peter Shire from the United States, Hans Hollein from Austria, Xavier Mariscal from Spain, and Shiro Kuramata and Arata Isozaki from Japan.
