# Design Promotion, Profession, and Management The nineteenth and early twentieth centuries had seen the proliferation of many societies which sought to promote design as a significant activity, whether from a social, cultural, political, or economic point of view [114].
These included such diverse bodies as the Swedish Society ofIndustrial Design (Svenska Slödförinengen), which had been established in 1845to encourage and promote handicraft in industry, the Finnish Society of Crafts and Design of 1879, the Art Workers' Guild and the Arts and Crafts Exhibition Society, founded in Britain in 1884 and 1888 respectively, the Hungarian Decorative Art Society (Magyar Iparmüvészeti Társulat) of 1885, the Polish Applied Arts Society of 19o1, the Netherlands Association for Crafts and Industrial Art (Nederlandsche Vereeniging voor Ambachts en Nijverheidskunst), the Danish Society of Arts and Crafts and Industrial Design of 1907, and the National Alliance of Art and Industry in the United States of 1912.
The more significant impact inGermany of the activities of the Deutscher Werkbund in the twenty-five years following its foundation in 1907 has already been discussed in Chapters 1 and 2.
Like other similar less influential bodies formed in its wake, such as the British Design and Industries Association (DIA) founded in 1915, it sought to achieve recognition of the economic significance of design in manufacturing industry and thus indirectly helped to enhance the professional status of the designer.
The long-term difficulties inherent in efforts to achieve recognition of the design profession in manufacturing industry can be seen to reside partially in the conflict between notions of the individual artistic personality and the economic and technological realities of manufacturing industry.
Indeed, for much of the twentieth century there has been considerable reservation about designers in manufacturing circles.
Several instances of such suspicion early in the century can be detected in the debates about design and standardization in industry.
But there was also considerable disagreement amongst designers and their propagandists themselves, centering on opposition of individuality and standardization, subjectivity and objectivity, and artistic licence and social responsibility.

Although these rifts have been accorded a high historicalprofile on account of the dominance of personality-centred design-historical writing, resonances of such concerns were felt in many countries in the boardrooms of everyday manufacturing industry, where the spectre of the artistic personality, oblivious of the realities of production technology, consumer preferences and market strategies, haunted the thoughts of many industrialists and businessmen.
In Britain, for example, the Council for Art and Industry's 1937 report on Design and the Designer in British Industry stated that a key ‘difficulty which has to be overcome is the·existence of a mutual distrust between artists and manufacturers'.1 Design-historical writing has often obscured much of the wider picture of the professional status of the designer and the role of design in industry through its generalfocus on successful partnerships between designers and industry: attention is drawn to Sweden, for example, whereWilhelm Kåge worked as Art Director for the ceramics manufacturer Gustavsberg, and Simon Gate and Edward Hald helped to establish the artistic reputation of the glass manufacturer Orrefors; to Italy, where Marcello Nizzoli designed for Olivetti and Gio Ponti for the ceramics manufacturer Richard-Ginori and the furniture maker Fontana; and to the United States, where the first generation of industrial designers such as Raymond Loewy, Henry Dreyfuss, and Walter Dorwin Teague worked for large, well-known corporate clients.
However, the widely-felt uncertainty of the connotations of terms commonly usedin the interwar years such ascommercial art' or graphic design',‘industrial art'or‘industrial design'reflected the inability of designers to establish a clearcut professional identity or status.
Indeed, the use of such terms can lend insights into the changing politics of professional validation.
For example, the Chartered Society of Designers (CSD) in London, the leading professional organization for designers in Britain, began life in 193o as the Society of Industrial Artists (SIA) and despite a radical reorganization at the end of the Second World War, did not actually incorporate the word design' into its title until 1965, when it became the Societyof IndustrialArtists and Designers.
The last change of title, to Chartered Society of Designers, reflected the continuing drive for more widespread professional recognition by the business and manufacturing community: the omission of the word 'Artists', together with any notion of the idiosyncracies that such a term might still have been thought to imply, was perhaps as significant as the word Chartered', with all of its professional connotations. ## The design profession in interwar Britain The Society of Artists (SIA) was established with two distinct aims, the first of which was to establish thè profession of the Designer on a sounder basis by forming a controlling authority to advance and protect the interests of all engaged in the production of Design for Industry, Publishing and Advertising.
The second purpose is to assemble the resources of Design as a vital factor in British Industry and so to assist the advancement of British Trade, both at home and abroad.² The founding of the SIA appeared to presage a flurry of official design activity in Britain in succeeding years: a Board of Trade Committee under the Chairmanship of Lord Gorell was appointed in the following year, 1931, to report on Art and Industry;³ late in 1933 the Council for Art and Industry was established under the auspices of the Board of Trade, making its mark through the publication of a number of designcentred reports (including Design and the Designer of 1937), the organization of exhibitions, liaison with industry, and involvement in design education; in 1936 a National Register of Industrial Art Designers was established, and the Royal Society of Arts appointed ten members to act as a new select faculty of Designers for Industry (upgraded by the Home Office to Royal Designers for Industry (RDI) in the following year).
It was also a decade which heralded a number of BBCradio programmes devoted to design matters, saw the publication of Herbert Read's celebrated pro-modernist text, Art and Industry (1934),4 and Nikolaus Pevsner's Pioneers of the Modern Movement (1936)⁵ and An Enquiry into Industrial Art in England (1937),6 as well as the mounting of a number of widely reported exhibitions7 including the large-scale British Art in Industry'show at the Royal Academy, with 2,223 exhibits on show.
The Design and Industries Association (DIA) was also active at the time in its promotion of the design cause, publishing its own magazines, newsletters, books, and other design propaganda, as wellas organizing public exhibitions.
But, as has been suggested in Britain and Modernism' in Chapter 2, such apparently clear-cut evidence, though cited by many historians of British design,8 can be misleading in its portrayal of a real sea change in terms of recognition of the designer in industry.
The long history of British design has often embraced the establishment of many official design-related committees, particularly in times of economic difficulty, when the government or its agencies wish to be seen to be working towards improving the position.
An early instance of this was the Select Committee on Art and Manufactures which was appointed in 1836 in the light of worries about increasingly strong competition from foreign manufacturers.
Almost 15o years later, in not dissimilar circumstances, and indicating in some ways how little the picture had really changed, the Department of Education and Science and the Design Council commissioned a report on The Industrial Design Requirements of Industry which came to the conclusion that foreign companies generally value more highly the contribution made by design to market success, they spend more money on industrial design and give the designer considerably more say in the final selection of the design for manufacture.
As a result they appear to attract and motivate designers who are generally very competent and highly creative.9 The DIA has left a considerable legacy for historians in its Yearbooks, magazines, and the prolific writings of its key members.
None the less, its influence should not be overestimated: its attitude to industrial design in the 192os was ambivalent, its membership was small by comparison with the Deutscher Werkbund(in 193o Werkbund membership stood at 3,ooo and the DIA's at 82o), and its magazines had an extremely difficult time in the difficult economic climate of the 193os.
Design in Industry launched in 1932 lasted for only two issues, the lifespan of Trend in Everyday Things of 1936 was equally brief, with only Design for Today lasting for any length of time, between 1933 and 1935.
Similarly, although the state-funded Council for Art and Industry, and its predecessor the British Institute ofIndustrial Art (founded in 192o), may be researched easily through their published reports and the papers lodged in the Public Record Office at Kew, their contemporary impact is more difficult to evaluate.
The Design and the Designer in Industry report of 1937, claimed by one British historian to be ‘by far the most influential'10of the Council for Art and Industry's publications, may well have been read in official circles, but the publisher, His Majesty's Stationery Office, was concerned a year after issue that only 1,594 of its 5,000 print run had been purchased.

Multidisciplinary design consultancies along the lines of the American model, as discussed in Chapter 3, were slow to emerge in Britain.
Early instances included the Bassett-Gray Group of Artists and Writers which became the Industrial Design Partnership (1935-40).
The founders were Milner Gray, Misha Black, and Walter Landor: Gray and Black were significantly involved in the establishment of the Design Research Unit (DRU) in 1943, a consultancy aimed at providing the necessary design expertise for postwar industry; Landor founded the internationally renowned consultancy, Landor Associates, in San Francisco in 1941 after emigrating to the United States in the previous year.

Before its closure in 1939 its clients includedAllied Ironfounders, Electrolux, General ElectricCompany, and Rootes, the motor manufacturers. ## The design profession: fresh opportunities after 1945 The post-1945 period saw many significant shifts in design practice and approach: fresh attitudes were engendered by the implications of changing technologies, which were in turn stimulated by widespread European and American involvement in the Second World War; by an increasing reconsideration of the extent to which the methods and approaches adopted by designers in the interwar years were still appropriate for postwar needs; and by the growing perception indesign circles that there was a fresh opportunity to establish in business circles a clearer perception of the importance, or professional status, of the designer than hadgenerally been the case in the 192os and 193os.11 There was a clear move away from faith in the primacy of intuition and individuality towards the recognition that the increasingly complex problems of an increasingly technologically-dependent society could only be solved by a coalition of a range of skills and expertise.
Ironically, the individual celebrity status which a number of the emergent breed of industrial designers in the United States had sought to cultivate in the 193os, inorder to gain both public recognition and commercial advantage, tended to reinforce doubts in business circles about individual designers'abilities to grasp the wider manufacturing, marketing, advertising, retailing, social, and economic implications of product development.
The principles of teamwork upon which the larger American practices were in fact organized pointed towards the basis upon which many postwar consultancies were to be organized.
This notion of teamwork had been strengthened by the wartime experience of many designers who worked alongside engineers and scientists in the war effort, gaining a more complete understanding ofnew materials and processes.
Examples of this multidisciplinary approach may be seen in consultancies such as the Japanese GK Industrial Design Associates which, from its modest beginnings in 1957, has grown to embrace product design, communication design, architectural and environmental design, research, planning and development, and design studies; Pentagram, founded in London in 1972 from a range of different types of expertise capable of catering for the complex demands of large corporations which were placing an increasing premium on design, was similarly able to deal with a broad range of commissions.
Other examples include Total Design, founded in the Netherlands in 1963, Minale Tattersfield, founded in Britain in 1964, and Plan Créatif, founded in Paris in 1985. ## State initiatives in design promotion The potential of design as a means of gaining a market edge was acknowledged by a number of governments in their establishment of official design bodies to promote its value to industry and the public.
The most significant of these was the Council of Industrial Design (COID) established in London in 1944 on an initial grant of £55,ooo, ready to assist in the anticipatedpostwar export drive.
Its specific aims were to promote good design in industry; to disseminate design advice and information to manufacturers, government departments, and others; to organize design exhibitions and advise on design education and training; and to educate the public in the importance of good design.
Amongst its most important early initiatives was the organization of the 1946 Britain Can Make It Exhibition.
Providing a sweeping vista ofthe benefits that design could provide for the home, work, and leisure this large-scale exhibition in theVictoria and Albert Museum attracted 1,432,369 visitors and included a display designed by Misha Black of the DRU entitled ‘What Industrial Design Means'.
Centred on the problem of designing an egg-cup for mass-production, Black showed how the industrial designer consulted with management, engineers, and salesmen in order to determine such matters as proportion, practicality, colour, and decoration, and how he needed to have an understanding of materials and mass-production technology.
As well as advising industry through its industrial officers and library and information service, the COID organized regional conferences and exhibitions, furnished show houses, commissioned reports, and published books and, from 1949 onwards, its own magazine, Design.
It has undergone many changes of fortune since then, often due to wider economic problems which at some times have threatened its grant-in-aid from government and at others have enhanced its profile, aswith the widely publicized design initiatives of the Conservative government under Mrs Thatcher in the 198os.
The COID established a Design Centrein central London in 1956, an information centre and showroomfor exhibitions of well-designed goods.
During the 196os its centre of gravity shifted away from consumer goods towards engineering, and in 1972 it was renamed the Design Council in recognition of its aim‘to wed engineering and industrial design in one body dedicated to improving design throughout all sections of British industry'.
In 1994 the Design Council was dramatically cut in size, resulting in the closure of the Design Centre and all regionaloffices, this leading to a complete re-appraisal of its role.12 Similar state-funded organizations were established in a number of other countries in the postwar years.
In Poland the Buiro Nadzoru Estetyki Producji (BNEP/Office for the Supervision of Aesthetic Production) was established in 1947 within the Ministry of Culture and Art under the directorship of Wanda Telakowska.
Its aim was to invigorate the design profession in the immediate postwar years, to improve standards of design in industry through the provision of models and prototypes, and to promote greater aesthetic awareness in everyday life.
However, its ambitions were soon thwarted by the Stalinist reorientation of the State of the late 194os.13 Later, in 196o, the Council of Design and Aesthetics ofIndustrial Production was established to promote industrial design activity although its effective impact was very limited.
Elsewhere in eastern Europe, state initiatives were equally problematic.
In the USSR, VNIITE(the All-Union Scientific Research Institute for Technical Aesthetics) was set up under the directorship of Yuri Soloviev in 1962, but its subsequent achievements were considerably constrained by high levels of official bureaucracy and planning.14 Its greatest strengths lay in ergonomics and engineering design.
In West Germany, the German Parliament voted in 1951 to establish a Rat für Formgebung (Design Council) which was inaugurated in 1953 to further the understanding of design in manufacture.
Although it received attention in the Milan Triennali it was not highly effective in the 196os and 197os, but with support from the Ministry of Economic Affairs it has played a continuing role in promoting greater awareness of design in German industry, a task of considerably greater complexity in the wake of the unification of the FDR and GDR.
In France, the picture of state support for design has been more ambivalent, although the state has provided architects and designers with many high-profile commissions which during the 198os included furniture at the Elysée Palace, furnishing of the French Embassy in Washington, the interior design of the Musée d'Orsay, and redecoration work at the Louvre and the Ministry of Finance.
The Centre de Création Industrielle (CCI), first established in 1969, was funded by the Ministry of Culture from 1973 in order to promote the cultural dimensions of French design.
In 1975 the Ministry of Industrial and Scientific Development set up the Conseil Superieur de la Création Esthétique Industrielle, to complement in economic terms theCCI's cultural role, but following widespread lack of interest and support, as well as antipathy to any notions of intervention from the industrial sector, was soon wound up.
Other European initiatives include the commissioning in 196o by the Irish Government's Exports Board of a panel of Scandinavian design experts to report on potential design strategies that the Republic might adopt.15 Their deliberations may be seen to have resulted in the Government's establishment of the Kilkenny Design Workshops (KDW) in1965.

In the United States the Society of Industrial Designers was established in New York in 1944, initially with fourteen members (including Loewy, Bel Geddes, Teague, Deskey, Van Doren, and Russel Wright) but growing to almost 1oo members by 1949.
It aimed to improve the vocation of industrial design, by providing the mechanism through which the members of the profession can agree and take action on every type of common problem' as well as to promote better standards of industrial design education and toimprove public and industrial understanding of their work.
In order to provide a showcase for many examples of their members' work from 1949 onwards it produced an annual publication entitled US Industrial Design.
In 1955 the organization changed its name to the American Society of Industrial Designers, merging in 1965 with two other bodies to become the Industrial Designers Society of America (IDSA).
According to an article in the American business magazine Fortune,16 by the late 195os there were about 3oo design consultancies in the United States, a fact that also reflected the growing number of in-house design departments in manufacturing industry.
Many of them increasingly moved into the broader arena of design management rather than dealing with products or graphic design in isolation.
Many of these changes can be followed in the pages of Industrial Design which, founded in 1954, was the leading magazine for design professionals in the United States; in 198o it was retitled ID Magazine of Industrial Design.
In Italy in the 195os the names of designers were important selling tools, but their role in industry was generally much more concerned with product aesthetics than with the wider concerns occupying American and, to an increasing extent, British consultancies.
In 1956 the Italian Association for Industrial Designers (ADI) was founded and many of their concernscan be discerned in the magazine Stile Industria which was launched in 1954 and edited by Alberto Roselli.
Published in Italian and English, it was an important vehicle for growing international interest in the theory and practice of Italian design, although it closed in the difficult economic climate of 1963.
In 1951 the Japan Advertising Artists Club was founded, followed by the Japanese Industrial Designers Association (JIDA) which was founded in the following year with 25 members.
It has grown considerably since then, in tandem with the growing recognition of design in manufacturing industry discussed in Chapter 5, with 6or full members in 1985 (284 being employed by companies, 258 being freelance, and 59 drawn from education).
It also published its own magazine Industrial Design, which by 1985 had about 3,ooo subscribers, although there were only about twenty subscribing from abroad.
Throughout the 195os and 196os many other national design associations were formed¹7 and there was an increased awareness of the wider international climate, stimulated by the growing number of professional design magazines and the holding of international conferences, as at Aspen, Colorado, from the early 195os.
The International Council of Societies of Industrial Design (ICSID) was established in 1957, mounting conferences biennially at various locations around the world, followed in 1963 by the International Council of Graphic Design Associations (ICOGRADA).
Designers of many nationalities increasingly shared many concerns, and in 1981 ICOGRADA, ICSID and IFI (the International Federation of Interior Designers) held a joint conference in Helsinki, examining design issues on a world scale.
# A rational approach to design: the Hochschule für Gestaltung at UIm: 1953–1969 Penny Sparke suggested in her book Consultant Design: While Germany sells design in the name of science, Italy in the name of art, Scandinavia in the name of craft and the USA in the name of business, all these national images of design were necessary strategies in the highly competitive markets of the immediate post war years.
The role of the designer was to help develop a marketing strategy which would give his product a special place in that market.18 The scientific, rational, and efficient outlook associated with German design in the later 195os, as typified in the austere aesthetic of Braun products of the period, was very much boundup with the innovative curriculum at the Hochschule für Gestaltung at Ulm (HfG).
Inge Scholl, whose brother and sister had been executed by the Nazis in 1943, wanted to establish a new international school of design as part of an express commitment to social and moral reconstruction in the postwar years.
In fact many German universities had shown considerable continuity in personnel and outlook with the prewar years, and so the envisaged school was intended to reflect a distinctive alternative personality.
Scholl established a foundation in 195o in order to bring the school into being, and funding was also secured from the US government, German federal and regional government, and the business sector in the Federal Republic.
Opening its doors in 1953 as a private institution, and thus free from the constraints of the local and national education system, its original impetus was focused on the idea of a new Bauhaus: visiting teachers included Johannes Itten, Josef Albers, and Mies van der Rohe, all of whom had contributed to the original Bauhaus ethos in the 192os.
In 1955 the HfG moved into new buildings designed by Max Bill, who had been installed as director in the previous year.
Students and staff were involved in their construction and furnishing, much as the Bauhaus students had been under Gropius at Dessau in the mid-192os.
However, within a short space of time ideological differences between Bill and several of his younger colleagues began to emerge.
These centred on their strong desire to emphasize the theoretical dimensions of the curriculum at the expense of the studio- and workshop-based studies upon which Bill placed a much higher priority.
He resigned as director in 1956, and the running of the school was placed in the hands of a rectoral board of five (later three) members, renewable on an annual basis.
From then there was an increasing emphasis on science and other disciplines which cast a fresh perspective on design thinking and practice, leading to Bill's resignation from the school in 1957.
His departure was also exacerbated by personal as well as curricular conflicts with Tomás Maldonado who had joined the staff in 1956.
Maldonado played a key role in theoretical debates, particularly through his many contributions to, and editorship of, the HfG's magazine Ulm, which was published between 1958 and 1959 and from 1962 to 1968.
Other HfG theorists included Claude Schnaidt and Giu Bonsiepe who, following the closure of the school, became an important figure in South American design.
Richard Hamilton, who had visited the HfG in 1959, put into words the distinctiveness of the institution thus: The major difference between the HfG and any other school of design in the world is precisely in the rejection ofthose principles which gave the Bauhaus its meaning and almost every school now available for the design student.19 By the later 195os mathematical, statistical, and analytical methods, together with sociology, anthropology, physical and behavioural psychology, and twentieth-century culturalhistory, were considered sufficiently important elements to rank alongside the more practical aspects of the curriculum.
In the early 196os methodology and theory assumed a dominant role in many areas of work, creating further tensions with those who placed a greater priority on the practical elements.
This state of relative ideological confusion led to the creation of a new constitution for the school in 1962, when once more its direction fell under the control of a single person, initially Otl Aicher.
It was also in this period that the radicalism of the institution came under attack in the press, accompanied by a funding crisis.

The first conference in the field, entitled ‘Systematic and Intuitive Methods in Engineering, Industrial Design, Architecture and Communications', was held at Imperial College, London, in 1962.

As a result many designers were eager to reconcile emergent disciplines such as ergonomics, anthropometrics, cybernetics, marketing, and management science with design thinking.
In such shifts away from the intuitive, micro- rather than macro-, means of problem-solving designers increasingly began to make use of interaction charts and matrices.
Such a rational outlook had its roots in the application of scientific techniques known as OR (Operational Research) which had been used for problem-solving in the Second World War, having evolved alongside developments in ergonomics.

Publishing the influential Measure of Man: Human Factors in Design (1959), Dreyfuss was important in this movement which developed alongside a desire in professional design circles to move away from individual, intuitive, and artistic approaches to design problems towards more collaborative and systematic solutions. in Designing for People26 of 1955 [119].
Another well-known text which examined the problem of designing for cleanliness and hygiene was Alexander Kira's The Bathroom Book.27First published in the United States in 1966, it was based upon a seven-year research project at Cornell University and included anthropometric, ergonomic, and many other factors in his analysis.
In some respects related to this growing concern for method and system in design was the emergent field of design management, which has been touched upon in relation to the increasing depth of expertise offered by consultancies.
In Britain it was recognized by the Royal Society of Arts in its Presidential Awards for Design Management, which were initiated in 1965: Heals, Hille, London Transport, and Conran were among the early recipients.
An early exponent in the field was Michael Farr, who set up his own design management consultancy, Michael Farr (Design Integration) Ltd., in Tottenham Court Road, London, writing Design Management28in 1966.
Increasingly many of the larger consultancies around the world have subsumed managerial, financial, and business analysis as an aspect of their work for large corporate clients. #
